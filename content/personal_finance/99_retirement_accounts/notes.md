# Traditional vs. Roth IRAs and 401(k)s

*General U.S. federal tax information, not personal tax or investment advice. Plan rules and your income can change the result.*

| Account | Contribution tax treatment | Withdrawal tax treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional IRA | May be deductible now, subject to income and workplace-plan rules | Generally taxable later |
| Roth IRA | Made with after-tax money | Qualified withdrawals are tax-free |
| Traditional 401(k) | Pre-tax salary deferral | Generally taxable later |
| Roth 401(k) | After-tax salary deferral | Qualified withdrawals are tax-free |

Collectively, these are **tax-advantaged retirement accounts** (or retirement plan/account types).

## Contributions

### What are the annual limits, and why do they differ?

Congress created IRAs and 401(k)s as separate parts of the tax code and assigned them different limits. A 401(k) is designed as a primary workplace retirement plan and is subject to employer-plan rules, including eligibility, reporting, fiduciary, and nondiscrimination requirements. An IRA is a portable individual account with fewer administrative requirements, but Congress allows much less tax-advantaged saving through it. There is no mathematical reason the limits must differ; it is a policy choice, and it means workers without a workplace plan generally have less tax-advantaged contribution room.

| Tax year | IRA limit* | 401(k) employee limit* |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2023 | $6,500 | $22,500 |
| 2024 | $7,000 | $23,000 |
| 2025 | $7,000 | $23,500 |
| 2026 | $7,500 | $24,500 |

*For 2026, people 50+ can generally add $1,100 to an IRA and $8,000 to a 401(k). If permitted by the plan, participants who turn 60–63 during 2026 can instead make an $11,250 401(k) catch-up. Beginning in 2026, participants whose prior-year wages from the plan sponsor exceeded $150,000 generally must make catch-up contributions on a Roth basis if the plan has a Roth feature. Employer contributions do not use the employee $24,500 limit, but a separate overall plan limit applies.*

An hourly employee is not barred from a 401(k); the employee can participate if the employer offers a plan and the employee satisfies its eligibility rules. Without an employer plan, the employee is generally limited to an IRA. A self-employed person may be able to establish a solo 401(k), SEP IRA, or another small-business retirement plan.

IRA contributions require compensation from work. For 2026, the maximum is the smaller of $7,500 ($8,600 if age 50+) or the person's taxable compensation for the year. Someone currently unemployed can still contribute based on wages or self-employment income earned earlier in the same year. Unemployment benefits, interest, dividends, and most rental income do not count as compensation for this purpose. A married person filing jointly may use the spousal IRA rules when the couple has enough combined compensation, even if that person personally had none.

### Can I split money among Roth and traditional accounts?

Yes. Traditional and Roth IRA contributions share one IRA limit. Traditional and Roth 401(k) salary deferrals share one 401(k) limit. The IRA and 401(k) limits are separate, so you can use both if eligible. A Roth 401(k) must be offered by your employer; a Roth IRA has income limits.

### If I earn $175,000, can I contribute directly to a Roth IRA?

It depends on your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI), not your salary alone. If your **2026 MAGI** is $175,000, you cannot contribute directly if you file single because eligibility phases out from $153,000 to $168,000. At that MAGI, you can contribute the full amount if married filing jointly because that phase-out is $242,000–$252,000. A $175,000 salary can produce a lower MAGI—for example, pre-tax traditional 401(k) deferrals generally reduce federal taxable wages—so a single filer earning that salary may still qualify.

### What if my employer does not offer a retirement plan?

If you are single and not covered by a workplace retirement plan, there is no MAGI phase-out for deducting an otherwise eligible traditional IRA contribution. For example, an hourly employee without a workplace plan can generally deduct a traditional IRA contribution up to the annual limit or taxable compensation earned that year, whichever is less.

The Roth IRA rules are different: their MAGI limits apply whether or not you have a workplace plan. For 2026, a single filer's permitted Roth IRA contribution is reduced from $153,000 to $168,000 of MAGI and eliminated at $168,000. If you are married and your spouse is covered by a workplace plan, separate MAGI limits can restrict your traditional IRA deduction even when you personally are not covered; for 2026, the joint-filer phase-out for that situation is $242,000–$252,000.

### If I max a traditional 401(k), does a traditional IRA still give me a tax break?

Usually not as an additional **current-year deduction** for a single filer earning about $175,000 and covered by a 401(k): the 2026 traditional-IRA deduction phases out from $81,000 to $91,000 of MAGI. Merely contributing to a 401(k) does not eliminate the IRA deduction; being covered by a workplace plan and having MAGI above the phase-out does.

That does not mean it never makes sense to max both accounts. After maxing a traditional 401(k), you may still benefit from a direct Roth IRA contribution if your MAGI permits it, because qualified growth and withdrawals can be tax-free. If your MAGI is too high for a direct Roth contribution, you can make a nondeductible traditional IRA contribution if otherwise eligible, but that is commonly useful mainly as the first step in a backdoor Roth IRA. The conversion's pro-rata tax calculation includes your year-end balances across all traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs, so existing pre-tax IRA money can make it partly taxable. Leaving a nondeductible contribution in a traditional IRA provides tax-deferred growth but no second deduction, and its earnings are generally taxed as ordinary income when withdrawn.

### Are these state or federal rules?

The contribution and federal-tax rules are federal. Your employer’s 401(k) can impose stricter plan rules. States may tax distributions differently.

### What happens if I contribute too much?

For an IRA, the usual penalty is a 6% excise tax each year on the excess until corrected. For a 401(k), contact payroll or the plan administrator promptly. An excess deferral generally must be distributed with attributable earnings by April 15 of the following year. Missing that deadline can cause the excess to be taxed both in the contribution year and again when eventually distributed.

### Can I roll a former employer’s 401(k) into Vanguard?

Usually, yes: directly roll a traditional 401(k) into a traditional IRA, or a Roth 401(k) into a Roth IRA. These like-to-like direct rollovers generally avoid current tax and the usual 20% withholding, and they do not count against annual contribution limits. Moving untaxed traditional 401(k) money to a Roth IRA is generally taxable even when done directly. Also, time in a Roth 401(k) generally does not count toward a Roth IRA's five-year period; an existing Roth IRA's clock applies, or a new clock begins if this is your first Roth IRA.

## Withdrawals

### When can I withdraw without the 10% early-withdrawal tax?

| Account | Basic rule |
| --- | --- |
| Traditional IRA | Age 59½; income tax usually still applies. |
| Traditional 401(k) | Age 59½, subject to plan distribution rules; income tax usually applies. |
| Roth IRA | Your regular contributions can generally come out anytime. Earnings are tax-free only in a qualified withdrawal. |
| Roth 401(k) | A qualified distribution generally requires age 59½ (or death/disability) and its 5-year rule. |

Some exceptions can remove the 10% tax, including disability, qualifying medical expenses, certain IRA education or first-home withdrawals, and separation from an employer during or after the calendar year in which you turn 55 for distributions from that employer's 401(k). The exceptions differ by account type, and the age-55 exception does not apply to IRAs.

### Does the Roth five-year rule still apply after age 59½?

Yes. For a Roth IRA, tax-free earnings generally require both a qualifying event—most commonly reaching age 59½—and five tax years beginning January 1 of the first tax year for which you contributed to any Roth IRA. Each conversion can also have a separate five-year period for determining whether the 10% additional tax applies. Roth 401(k)s have a separate five-year clock.

### What happens if I withdraw early?

Traditional IRA and 401(k) withdrawals are generally taxable income plus a 10% additional tax before age 59½, unless an exception applies. Roth IRA ordering rules treat regular contributions as coming out first, so they are generally tax- and penalty-free; conversion amounts come next and earnings last. Earnings from a nonqualified withdrawal may be taxable and subject to the 10% tax. The taxable portion of a conversion is not taxed as income again when withdrawn, but it may face the 10% tax if withdrawn within that conversion's five-year period and no exception applies. A nonqualified Roth 401(k) withdrawal generally contains a proportional share of contributions and earnings, and its earnings portion can be taxable and subject to the 10% tax.

### Do required minimum distributions apply?

Traditional IRAs and traditional 401(k)s generally require minimum distributions later in life. Roth IRAs and designated Roth 401(k) accounts do not require lifetime distributions from the original owner, although beneficiaries are subject to inherited-account distribution rules.

### What is the practical withdrawal process?

Request a distribution from the IRA custodian or 401(k) plan administrator. For money you want to keep invested, request a **direct rollover** instead of a cash payment. Keep the tax form (usually Form 1099-R) and confirm any exception with a tax professional.
